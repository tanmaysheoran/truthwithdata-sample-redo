Is Renting Really 'Dead Money' in the UK? What the Data Shows

Data Journalism October 23, 2026 18 min read
The short answer
Mortgage interest is dead money too. Buying wins only if house prices rise.

At September 2026 rates, the first-year interest on a 90% mortgage for the average home is 77–107% of the rent on that home, and once maintenance, insurance and the return the deposit could have earned are added, owning costs more than renting in all 12 UK regions. Buying comes out ahead through house-price growth: if prices keep rising at their 2005–2026 pace of 3.0% a year, buying beats renting after 1.4 years (Scotland) to 4.1 years (South East); if prices stay flat, it takes 11 years in Scotland, 18–27 years in six other regions and more than 30 years in the other five. Mortgage rates matter almost as much: one percentage point higher stretches the baseline to 2.3–12.2 years. In our 2005–2026 backtest, buyers who sold after five years were ahead of an equivalent renter in 79% of cases, but most who bought in 2006–08 were still behind after five years, and most 2022–24 buyers were behind by 2026. To run your own numbers, use the calculator.

"Renting is dead money" is the most repeated piece of financial advice in Britain. It is half right. Rent buys you nothing you can sell later. But a mortgage is not a savings account either: most of the early payments are interest, and stamp duty, solicitors, surveys, estate agents, repairs and insurance are just as unrecoverable as rent. The honest question is not "rent or own?" but "which set of unrecoverable costs is smaller, for the same home, over the time I will live in it?" We answered it with official data for every UK region and 316 local authorities, and tested the answer against what actually happened to buyers and renters from 2005 to 2026.

1. What "dead money" leaves out

The phrase compares the whole of a rent payment with the whole of a mortgage payment and notices that only one of them leaves you owning something. That comparison makes three mistakes. It counts capital repayment as a benefit of buying while ignoring that the deposit used to get the mortgage could have been earning a return instead. It ignores the costs only owners pay: stamp duty (or LBTT in Scotland and LTT in Wales), conveyancing, a survey, mortgage product fees, maintenance, buildings insurance, service charges on flats and, eventually, an estate agent. And it quietly assumes the home will be worth more when you sell it, which is the one part of the calculation nobody can know in advance.

The fair comparison takes one home and two households. One buys it. The other rents the same home and invests the money the buyer put into the deposit and buying costs. Each month, whichever household has the lower housing bill invests the difference, so both spend exactly the same. At any point we can ask who is wealthier: the buyer, with the home's value minus the mortgage still owed and the cost of selling, or the renter, with their investments. The month from which the buyer stays ahead is the break-even point. That is the number this article is about.

2. How we compared renting and buying

The homes. For each of the 12 UK regions and nations we take the average price from the HM Land Registry UK House Price Index for July 2026 (the latest month available) and the average monthly private rent for the same month from the ONS Price Index of Private Rents (PIPR). Both series are published by property type, so we also compare like with like: terraced with terraced, flats with flats. Both are published for local authorities in England and Wales, which gives us 316 local comparisons. We do not report a single "UK average" comparison: the UK average rent is weighted towards the rental stock, which is concentrated in London and cities, while the UK average price is weighted by sales, so their ratio describes no real home.

The money. All amounts are nominal pounds. The main scenario is a first-time buyer with a 10% deposit, paying the Bank of England's average quoted rate for a two-year fixed mortgage at 90% loan-to-value in September 2026 (5.37%), re-fixed every two years with a new product fee, on a 25-year repayment mortgage. We also run a 25% deposit at the 75% LTV rate (5.13%). The renter invests at 5% a year, the Financial Conduct Authority's intermediate projection rate for tax-advantaged products such as ISAs. House prices and rents grow at England's average over July 2005 to July 2026: 3.0% a year for prices and 3.0% a year for rents. That window is the longest both series cover, and it contains a crash, a decade of near-zero interest rates, a pandemic boom and the 2022–23 rate shock. Every one of these assumptions is varied in section 7.

3. Year one: interest versus rent

Start with the simplest version of the question: in the first year, what does owning cost that you will never get back, and how does it compare with rent? Figure 1 stacks the unrecoverable costs of owning the average home in each region with a 10% deposit: mortgage interest, maintenance and insurance, and the 5% return forgone on the deposit and buying costs. Capital repayment is left out because it comes back as equity.

Mortgage interest alone is between 77% of the rent (Scotland) and 107% (South East). In five regions, the East Midlands, West Midlands, East of England, South East and Wales, a first-time buyer's interest bill in year one is larger than the rent they would have paid. Add maintenance, insurance and the forgone return on the deposit and owning costs 5% (Scotland) to 45% (South East) more than renting, everywhere. On these numbers rent is no more "dead" than mortgage interest; it is somewhat less.

What turns the comparison around is the black tick. If the home gains 3.0% in value over the year, that gain, worth more than a year's maintenance and forgone return combined, brings the owner's net cost down to 56–77% of the rent. Buying wins on the expectation of price growth, and on nothing else. Before that growth, it is the more expensive way to live in the same home.

5. Findings by local authority

Regional averages blend cities and countryside with very different rent-to-price ratios. Figure 3 repeats the baseline calculation for the 316 local authorities in England and Wales for which both HM Land Registry and ONS publish July 2026 figures.

The pattern is driven almost entirely by the gross rental yield, rent as a share of price: across authorities the correlation between yield and break-even is −0.77. In 181 authorities (57%) buying the average home beats renting within five years. In 55 it takes more than ten, and in 15 the buyer is still behind after 30 years. The fastest are cities where rents are high relative to prices: Newcastle upon Tyne (1.0 years, yield 7.0%), Manchester, Portsmouth, Bristol and Southampton (1.1–1.2 years). The 15 that never break even are expensive rural and commuter districts with yields of 2.7–3.5%, including Elmbridge, Waverley and Mole Valley in Surrey, the Derbyshire Dales, South Hams, Malvern Hills and Powys. London spans almost the whole range, from Tower Hamlets (1.3 years) and Newham (1.5) to Kingston upon Thames (19), Kensington and Chelsea (27.5) and Richmond upon Thames (29.75).

Local averages compare different homes

At local authority level the "average home" for sale and the "average home" for rent can be quite different: rented stock in a city is mostly flats and terraces, while sales in a rural district include many detached houses. Comparing like with like reduces the gap. Using terraced houses only, the median break-even across authorities falls from 4.25 to 2.25 years; using flats, also 2.25. The ranking of authorities is similar (rank correlation 0.74), but the all-types figures, especially the "never" cases, are probably biased towards renting.

6. Historical backtest, 2005–2026

A forecast with one assumed growth rate cannot show what happens when prices fall, rates jump or rents surge. So we replayed history. For every region and every July from 2005 (2009 for Wales, 2011 for Scotland, 2015 for Northern Ireland, when their rent series start) we simulated a buyer and a renter of the region's average home, using the actual monthly prices, actual rents, and the actual 2-year fixed rate at each re-fix. The buyer has a 25% deposit, because the 75% LTV rate series is the only one complete back to 2005. The renter invests either at 5% a year or at Bank Rate, a proxy for the best cash savings rate. That gives 232 purchase dates and 2,418 buy-and-sell pairs in each version.

Three findings stand out. First, buying usually won, and won big over long holds. Holding for five years, the buyer finished ahead in 79% of cases; holding for ten, 86%; and every 20-year hold in the data (18 of them, all England) ended with the buyer ahead. When the renter's alternative is cash at Bank Rate, which was 0.1–0.75% from 2009 to 2021, buying wins even more often: 83% of five-year holds and 98% of ten-year holds.

Second, timing can override everything. A buyer in July 2007 who sold five years later was behind the renter by between 4% of the purchase price (London) and 29% (North East); it took them 6 years (London) to 19 years (North East) to get ahead. Buyers in July 2022, 2023 and 2024 were behind an equivalent renter in 8 of 12 regions by July 2026, by a median of 2–5% of the price: they bought near a price peak, just as mortgage rates jumped.

Third, short holds are close to a coin toss. Selling after one year, the buyer was ahead in 47% of cases, and after two years in 60%. Stamp duty, fees and the estate agent take most of the first year's equity, so short-term outcomes depend on whether prices happened to rise in that particular year.

The backtest flatters buyers in one way

A renter who put the deposit into a global equity fund from 2009 to 2021 would probably have earned considerably more than our 5% assumption, which would shrink the buyer's lead in those years. 7. Sensitivity: interest rates, house prices, years held

Every number above rests on assumptions, so we varied each one, running all 12 regions for every combination and reporting the median break-even. Figure 5 crosses the two assumptions that matter most: the mortgage rate and house-price growth.

House-price growth dominates. At today's rate, the median break-even goes from 2.8 years with 3.0% growth to 8.0 years with 2%, 19 years with 1% and 26 years with none. No other input moves the answer that much. Interest rates come second. With 3.0% growth, a 4% mortgage rate gives a median break-even of 1.3 years; 6% gives 4.9 years; 7% gives 13. The reason is leverage: with a 10% deposit, the interest rate applies to a loan nine times the size of the buyer's stake.

The renter's return matters more than people expect. At 2% (roughly a good cash rate in real terms), the median break-even is 2.2 years; at 8% (the FCA's higher projection rate) the median buyer never stays ahead within 30 years, because the renter's compounding catches up in the later decades. Rent growth matters less when prices are rising: from 1% to 5% a year, the baseline break-even moves only from 3.0 to 2.7 years. It matters a great deal when prices are not rising: with 2% price growth, rents rising 1% a year mean the buyer never catches up, and 5% a year brings break-even down to 5.8 years.

Maintenance and deposit size are secondary. Halving maintenance to 0.5% of value cuts the median break-even from 2.8 to 2.0 years; raising it to 1.5% lifts it to 4.9 (8.3 in the slowest region). A bigger deposit barely matters in the model: 5% gives 2.9 years and 40% gives 2.6. In real life a bigger deposit matters more than this, because it unlocks cheaper rates and smaller monthly payments, and below a 10% deposit many buyers cannot borrow at all.

8. What this means if you are deciding

Rent is not dead money, and neither is buying a sure thing. Rent pays for somewhere to live with no maintenance bills, no transaction costs and the freedom to move. Mortgage interest pays for the same thing plus a leveraged bet on house prices. Over the last 20 years that bet mostly paid off. Whether it pays off for you depends on three things, in this order:

  • How long you will stay. Under two years, buying usually loses: our backtest gives the buyer roughly a coin-toss chance over one to two years, and the forward model has the buyer behind after two years in 11 of 12 regions. Beyond five years, buying won in 79% of historical cases and wins in every region at baseline assumptions.
  • What happens to house prices. If you think prices will roughly track the last 20 years, buying wins quickly almost everywhere. If you think they will stagnate, buying takes decades to win, or never does, at today's rates.
  • The local rent-to-price ratio. Where rents are high relative to prices, in most cities and for terraced houses and flats, buying wins faster. In expensive rural and commuter areas and for detached houses, renting is often the cheaper way to live in the same home.

One part of the "dead money" argument is right, and it is behavioural: the model assumes the renter actually invests the deposit and every month's saving. A renter who spends that money instead ends up with nothing, and a mortgage is a form of forced saving. If you rent, the comparison only holds if you automate the investing.

9. Limitations

  • Averages are not homes. Our prices are averages of homes sold and our rents averages of homes let. The two populations differ in size, age and location, most of all at local authority level. Comparing by property type narrows the gap but does not close it.
  • The forward model uses one constant growth rate. Real prices move in cycles, and the backtest shows that the year you buy can matter more than the average. The constant mortgage rate is equally unrealistic; it is a scenario, not a forecast.
  • Tax is simplified. We assume the renter's investments sit in an ISA and are tax-free. The ISA allowance is £20,000 a year, so a London deposit (£55,000) would take three tax years to shelter; outside an ISA, taxes on returns would favour buying. The backtest applies today's stamp duty rules to past purchases, which understates the stamp duty most buyers actually paid before 2014.
  • Some assumptions are thinly sourced. The 1%-of-value maintenance rule is a rule of thumb, not a measured average, and it may overstate maintenance where land is a large share of value (London). Service charges come from one estate agency's index for England and Wales and are applied to Scotland and Northern Ireland too. The product fee figure dates from February 2024. Each is varied or editable in the calculator.
  • Mortgage rates. Bank of England quoted rates are averages of advertised rates, not what any individual is offered. The 90% LTV series starts only in 2008 and has gaps, so the backtest uses 75% LTV. We had only 2-year fixed rates; many buyers choose 5-year fixes.
  • Not included: moving costs, furnishing, ground rent, leasehold extensions, the cost of a renter being asked to leave, rent controls, government schemes, mortgage-free years after the 25-year term (they fall outside most horizons) and anything non-financial: security, control over the home, flexibility. The model counts money, not wellbeing.
  • Northern Ireland prices are quarterly and its rents lag by two months; Scotland's rents are not published at council level, so it is missing from the local analysis.

def banded(price, bands):
    """bands: list of (upper threshold, rate %) applied to the slice below each threshold."""
    tax, lower = 0.0, 0.0
    for upper, rate in bands:
        if price > lower:
            tax += (min(price, upper) - lower) * rate / 100
        lower = upper
    return tax

INF = float("inf")
SDLT = [(125000, 0), (250000, 2), (925000, 5), (1500000, 10), (INF, 12)]           # England & NI
SDLT_FTB = [(300000, 0), (500000, 5)]                                                 # price <= £500k
LBTT = [(145000, 0), (250000, 2), (325000, 5), (750000, 10), (INF, 12)]               # Scotland
LBTT_FTB = [(175000, 0), (250000, 2), (325000, 5), (750000, 10), (INF, 12)]
LTT = [(225000, 0), (400000, 6), (750000, 7.5), (1500000, 10), (INF, 12)]             # Wales, no FTB relief

def transaction_tax(price, nation, first_time_buyer=True):
    if nation in ("england", "ni"):
        if first_time_buyer and price <= 500000:
            return banded(price, SDLT_FTB)
        return banded(price, SDLT)
    if nation == "scotland":
        return banded(price, LBTT_FTB if first_time_buyer else LBTT)
    if nation == "wales":
        return banded(price, LTT)
    raise ValueError(nation)

def survey_cost(price):
    xs, ys = zip(*A["survey_points"])
    return float(np.interp(price, xs, ys))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. The model: one monthly simulation gives buyer and renter wealth at every horizon
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def simulate(value, rent, mrate, ret, cidx, deposit_pct, nation, is_flat, region_code,
             ftb=True, maint_pct=None):
    """value[m], cidx[m] for m = 0..M; rent[m], ret[m] for m = 1..M (rent[0], ret[0] unused);
    mrate[m] = quoted 2-year fixed rate (%) available at the end of month m.
    Returns (buyer_wealth, renter_wealth) arrays of length M+1."""
    M = len(value) - 1
    P = value[0]
    if maint_pct is None:
        maint_pct = A["maintenance_flat_pct"] if is_flat else A["maintenance_house_pct"]
    sc = 0.0
    if is_flat:
        sc = A["service_charge_london"] if region_code == "E12000007" else A["service_charge_ew"]
    ins = 0.0 if is_flat else A["buildings_insurance"]
    conv = A["conveyancing_flat"] if is_flat else A["conveyancing_house"]

    deposit = P * deposit_pct / 100
    loan = P - deposit
    buy_costs = transaction_tax(P, nation, ftb) + (conv + A["product_fee"]) * cidx[0] + survey_cost(P)
    renter = deposit + buy_costs          # the renter invests the cash the buyer spends up front
    buyer = 0.0                           # buyer's side pot (invested when owning is cheaper)
    bal, n_left = loan, A["term_years"] * 12
    i = mrate[0] / 1200
    pmt = bal * i / (1 - (1 + i) ** -n_left)

    def sell_cost(v, c):
        return v * A["agent_fee_pct"] / 100 + A["conveyancing_sell"] * c

    bw = np.empty(M + 1); rw = np.empty(M + 1)
    bw[0] = value[0] - bal - sell_cost(value[0], cidx[0]); rw[0] = renter
    for m in range(1, M + 1):
        g = (1 + ret[m] / 100) ** (1 / 12)
        renter *= g; buyer *= g
        pay = 0.0
        if bal > 0:
            interest = bal * i
            principal = min(pmt - interest, bal)
            pay = interest + principal
            bal -= principal; n_left -= 1
            if bal < 1e-6:
                bal = 0.0
        out = pay + maint_pct / 100 * value[m - 1] / 12 + (ins + sc) / 12 * cidx[m]
        if m % A["fix_months"] == 0 and bal > 0 and m < M:   # re-fix: new rate, product fee
            i = mrate[m] / 1200
            pmt = bal * i / (1 - (1 + i) ** -n_left)
            out += A["product_fee"] * cidx[m]
        diff = out - rent[m]
        if diff > 0:
            renter += diff
        else:
            buyer -= diff
        bw[m] = value[m] - bal - sell_cost(value[m], cidx[m]) + buyer
        rw[m] = renter
    return bw, rw

def breakeven_years(bw, rw):
    """Break-even in years: the first month from which the buyer stays ahead of the renter for the
    rest of the horizon (NaN if the buyer is behind at the end). Using the last crossing rather than
    the first stops a brief lead (e.g. just before a re-fix fee or a price fall) counting as break-even."""
    behind = np.nonzero(bw[1:] < rw[1:])[0]
    if len(behind) == 0:
        return 1 / 12
    if behind[-1] == len(bw) - 2:
        return np.nan
    return (behind[-1] + 2) / 12

def forward_paths(P, R0, rate_pct, g_price, g_rent, r_inv, years=None):
    years = years or A["horizon_years"]
    M = years * 12
    m = np.arange(M + 1)
    value = P * (1 + g_price / 100) ** (m / 12)
    rent = R0 * (1 + g_rent / 100) ** ((m - 1) // 12)          # tenancy re-set once a year
    mrate = np.full(M + 1, rate_pct)
    ret = np.full(M + 1, r_inv)
    cidx = (1 + A["cost_inflation_pct"] / 100) ** (m / 12)
    return value, rent, mrate, ret, cidx

def run_forward(P, R0, rate_pct, g_price, g_rent, r_inv, deposit_pct, nation, is_flat, code, **kw):
    v, r, mr, rt, c = forward_paths(P, R0, rate_pct, g_price, g_rent, r_inv)
    return simulate(v, r, mr, rt, c, deposit_pct, nation, is_flat, code, **kw)

# --- Transaction taxes in force in October 2026 -------------------------------
banded <- function(price, upper, rate) {
  lower <- c(0, head(upper, -1))
  sum(pmax(0, pmin(price, upper) - lower) * rate / 100)
}
transaction_tax <- function(price, nation, ftb = TRUE) {
  if (nation %in% c("england", "ni")) {
    if (ftb && price <= 500000) return(banded(price, c(300000, 500000), c(0, 5)))
    return(banded(price, c(125000, 250000, 925000, 1500000, Inf), c(0, 2, 5, 10, 12)))
  }
  if (nation == "scotland") {
    nil <- if (ftb) 175000 else 145000
    return(banded(price, c(nil, 250000, 325000, 750000, Inf), c(0, 2, 5, 10, 12)))
  }
  if (nation == "wales") return(banded(price, c(225000, 400000, 750000, 1500000, Inf), c(0, 6, 7.5, 10, 12)))
  stop(nation)
}
survey_cost <- function(p) approx(survey_x, survey_y, xout = p, rule = 2)$y

# --- The model ------------------------------------------------------------------
# value, cidx: months 0..M (R index 1..M+1); rent, ret: month m stored at index m+1
simulate <- function(value, rent, mrate, ret, cidx, deposit_pct, nation, is_flat, code) {
  M <- length(value) - 1; P <- value[1]
  maint <- if (is_flat) A$maintenance_flat_pct else A$maintenance_house_pct
  sc <- if (is_flat) (if (code == "E12000007") A$service_charge_london else A$service_charge_ew) else 0
  ins <- if (is_flat) 0 else A$buildings_insurance
  conv <- if (is_flat) A$conveyancing_flat else A$conveyancing_house
  deposit <- P * deposit_pct / 100; bal <- P - deposit
  buy_costs <- transaction_tax(P, nation) + (conv + A$product_fee) * cidx[1] + survey_cost(P)
  renter <- deposit + buy_costs; buyer <- 0
  n_left <- A$term_years * 12; i <- mrate[1] / 1200
  pmt <- bal * i / (1 - (1 + i)^-n_left)
  sell_cost <- function(v, c) v * A$agent_fee_pct / 100 + A$conveyancing_sell * c
  bw <- rw <- numeric(M + 1)
  bw[1] <- value[1] - bal - sell_cost(value[1], cidx[1]); rw[1] <- renter
  for (m in 1:M) {
    g <- (1 + ret[m + 1] / 100)^(1 / 12); renter <- renter * g; buyer <- buyer * g
    pay <- 0
    if (bal > 0) {
      interest <- bal * i; principal <- min(pmt - interest, bal)
      pay <- interest + principal; bal <- bal - principal; n_left <- n_left - 1
      if (bal < 1e-6) bal <- 0
    }
    out <- pay + maint / 100 * value[m] / 12 + (ins + sc) / 12 * cidx[m + 1]
    if (m %% A$fix_months == 0 && bal > 0 && m < M) {
      i <- mrate[m + 1] / 1200; pmt <- bal * i / (1 - (1 + i)^-n_left)
      out <- out + A$product_fee * cidx[m + 1]
    }
    d <- out - rent[m + 1]
    if (d > 0) renter <- renter + d else buyer <- buyer - d
    bw[m + 1] <- value[m + 1] - bal - sell_cost(value[m + 1], cidx[m + 1]) + buyer
    rw[m + 1] <- renter
  }
  list(bw = bw, rw = rw)
}
breakeven_years <- function(s) {           # first month from which the buyer stays ahead
  behind <- which(s$bw[-1] < s$rw[-1]); n <- length(s$bw) - 1
  if (!length(behind)) return(1 / 12)
  if (max(behind) == n) return(NA)
  (max(behind) + 1) / 12
}
run_forward <- function(P, R0, rate, gp, gr, r_inv, dep, nation, is_flat, code) {
  M <- A$horizon_years * 12; m <- 0:M
  simulate(P * (1 + gp / 100)^(m / 12), R0 * (1 + gr / 100)^((m - 1) %/% 12), rep(rate, M + 1),
           rep(r_inv, M + 1), (1 + A$cost_inflation_pct / 100)^(m / 12), dep, nation, is_flat, code)
}

Conclusion: is renting dead money?

No more than mortgage interest is. For the average UK home at today's rates, the unrecoverable costs of owning in year one are higher than the rent in every region. Buying wins because house prices have tended to rise, and when they do, a buyer with a mortgage captures that rise on the whole value of the home. Over 2005–2026 that made buying the better financial choice in four out of five five-year holds.

But the answer depends on how long you stay, where you live and what prices do next. If prices keep rising 3% a year, buying the average home pays off within 1.4 to 4.1 years in every region. If prices go nowhere, it takes one to three decades or never pays off. Anyone who tells you renting is always dead money is assuming the second scenario can't happen; 2007–2012 and 2022–2026 say it can.

Key takeaways

Mortgage interest alone is 77–107% of the rent on the same home at September 2026 rates. Buying beats renting after 1.4–4.1 years if prices rise 3% a year, after 11 years to never if they are flat. Short stays favour renting; over five years, buyers came out ahead in 79% of historical cases. Terraced houses and city flats favour buying; detached homes and expensive rural districts favour renting. Try your own numbers in the Rent vs Buy Calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Is renting dead money?

No more than mortgage interest is. At September 2026 rates, first-year mortgage interest on a 90% mortgage for the average UK home is 77% to 107% of the rent on the same home, and once maintenance, insurance and the return a deposit could have earned are added, owning costs more than renting in every UK region. Buying comes out ahead through house-price growth: if prices rise 3% a year it beats renting within 1.4 to 4.1 years, depending on the region; if prices stay flat it takes 11 years to more than 30.

How long do you need to stay for buying to beat renting in the UK?

At September 2026 mortgage rates and with house prices rising 3% a year, buying the average home beats renting after 1.4 years in Scotland and up to 4.1 years in the South East, with a median of 2.8 years across 12 regions. History is less tidy: in our 2005 to 2026 backtest, buyers were ahead of an equivalent renter in 60% of two-year holds, 79% of five-year holds and 86% of ten-year holds.

Is mortgage interest dead money?

In the same sense as rent, yes: it pays for the use of money and is never returned. In the first year of a 90% mortgage at 5.37%, interest on the average home is larger than the rent on that home in the East Midlands, West Midlands, East of England, South East and Wales, and 77% to 98% of the rent in the other regions. Capital repayments are different: they come back as equity.

Is it better to rent or buy in London?

For the average London home, buying beats renting after 3.5 years if prices rise 3% a year, but stamp duty of about £17,500 on a £550,000 home makes selling within two years costly: after two years the buyer is about £14,400 behind. Flats break even in 1.8 years and detached houses not within 30 years. By borough, break-even ranges from 1.3 years in Tower Hamlets to almost 30 years in Richmond upon Thames.

What happens if house prices stay flat or fall?

With flat prices and today's mortgage rates, the median UK region needs 26 years for buying to beat renting, and in five regions (East Midlands, West Midlands, East of England, South East and Wales) the buyer is still behind after 30 years. Falling prices are worse: people who bought in July 2007 were behind an equivalent renter for between 6 years (London) and 19 years (North East).

Does a bigger deposit make buying better value?

Only a little in our model. Moving from a 10% to a 25% deposit, with the lower 75% loan-to-value mortgage rate, shortens break-even by 0.1 to 1.0 years. In practice a larger deposit matters more because it unlocks cheaper mortgage deals and smaller monthly payments, and many lenders will not lend with less than 10%.
